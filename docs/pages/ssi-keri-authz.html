The premise

Permissions become portable, verifiable authority.

The significant architectural shift is from permissions stored exclusively in application databases to portable, cryptographically verifiable authority evaluated against local policy.

Together, KERI and AuthZ can support identity, authority and delegation without relying entirely on centralised identity providers.

The receiving system still decides.

Authority as a credential

The holder can present evidence of its authority independently of the system that originally issued it. The receiving system still determines whether to accept that authority.

The stack

Six layers, from key control to evidence.

KERI and AuthZ are complementary components of a decentralised trust architecture. Each layer has one responsibility, and each depends on the one before it.

  1. 01 · KERI

    Verifiable identity and key control

    Autonomic identifiers and their key event logs establish cryptographic control and provenance.

  2. 02 · AuthN

    Authentication of the acting entity

    The request is signed by the acting identifier and is fresh, so it is bound to its sender and cannot be replayed.

  3. 03 · Credentials

    Verifiable attributes, roles and delegated authority

    ACDCs express and chain permissions and delegated authority. The TEL records issuance and revocation.

  4. 04 · AuthZ

    Determining permitted actions

    A deterministic evaluation of identity, authority, request, context, policy and time.

  5. 05 · Enforcement

    Applying authorisation decisions

    Decisions are applied at the interface, with default-deny whenever verification fails.

  6. 06 · Audit

    Recording evidence of decisions and actions

    Each decision and action leaves evidence that can be checked after the fact.

Proposed KERI-bound AuthZ architecture

From credential to decision.

Authority arrives as a credential, is evaluated by a deterministic function, and is reasoned about within a verified context graph.

AuthZ as a deterministic function

AuthZ = f(I,A,R,C,P,T).

Authorisation can be expressed as a function of six inputs. Given the same inputs, it always returns the same decision.

  • I

    Verified identity

  • A

    Delegated authority

  • R

    Requested resource and action

  • C

    Current context

  • P

    Applicable policy

  • T

    Temporal validity

Deterministic implementation

Every condition must hold.

A deterministic implementation might look like this. Any single failure denies the request.

This lends itself to formal verification using Lean, alongside a Verified Context Graph (VCG) representing the relevant authority and policy relationships.

KERI and Verified Context Graphs

What each relationship carries.

KERI establishes the verifiability of the actors and credentials within a graph. VCGs provide the semantic and logical structure for evaluating their authority.

  • 01

    Provenance

    Where the relationship came from, and who asserted it.

  • 02

    Conditions

    The constraints under which it holds.

  • 03

    Validity periods

    When it starts, and when it stops applying.

  • 04

    Evidence

    What supports it, verifiable by anyone who evaluates it.

Security considerations

Identity is not permission.

KERI does not, by itself, establish that an entity is trustworthy or that it has permission to perform a business action.

The architecture must also address

Six things to get right.

Where verifiable authority systems are most likely to fail, and where they need to be strict.

  • 01

    Trusted credential issuers and roots of authority

  • 02

    Revocation propagation and stale credential state

  • 03

    Request binding and replay prevention

  • 04

    Prevention of privilege escalation through delegation

  • 05

    Explicit resource, audience and contextual restrictions

  • 06

    Default-deny enforcement when verification fails

From identity to verifiable authority

Authority-centric security.

The larger opportunity is a transition from identity-centric security to authority-centric security.

Rather than asking

Who is this entity, and what roles have we assigned it?

We ask

Can this entity prove that it has the authority to perform this specific action, in this specific context, at this point in time?

For an economy increasingly populated by autonomous silicon intelligence, that distinction is fundamental.
